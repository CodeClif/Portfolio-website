I opened Tech Buddies randomly yesterday. There was no big plan behind it. I wanted to talk, a few people joined, and somewhere along the way it became one of the most useful Spaces I have hosted.

<a href="https://x.com/edemkumodzi" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Edem Kumodzi</a> joined. <a href="https://x.com/KiddBubu" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Bubu</a> joined too. Other founders and engineers came through, and the conversation kept moving between software, hardware, running a software business in Ghana, legal and certification questions, timelines, careers and distribution.

What I liked most was that it never became a room where technical people were trying to prove how technical they were. We could talk about software in a way that somebody who does not write code could still follow. That is probably the version of Tech Buddies I am most interested in.

## Distribution does not finish

The biggest thing I carried away was <Concept term="distribution" meaning="The work of getting a product in front of the people who might use it, and giving them a reason to try it, return to it or tell someone else about it." analogy="A great restaurant can have excellent food and still stay empty if nobody knows it exists or why they should visit." example="For a software product, distribution can be word of mouth, social posts, partnerships, search, communities, direct outreach or many other ways people discover and adopt it." />.

I used to think about distribution mostly as the stage after building. You build the thing, then you figure out how to get people to use it.

The conversation made that feel incomplete. Distribution does not really end. Even when a product is already known, the company is still reminding people it exists, reaching new people, entering new markets and finding better ways to explain why the product matters.

That changed the way I think about Pumply too. Shipping the app is not the finish line. Getting the first users is not the finish line either. Distribution is part of operating the product.

## Software exists outside the code

We also got into the less exciting parts of running a software company: when you need certain documents, what should exist before you start operating, what can happen while the business is growing and how founders actually navigate those decisions.

I did not leave the Space suddenly knowing every legal answer. That was not the useful part for me.

The useful part was remembering that a software business is still a business. The code can work perfectly and you can still have questions around regulation, paperwork, money, distribution, customers and operations. Building the product is only one system inside a much larger system.

## I think I finally understand what I want these conversations to feel like

I have listened to long conversations for years. I like when somebody starts with one question and the answer creates another question, and then that answer opens another door. Sometimes twenty minutes later they are somewhere nobody planned to go.

I never really thought of that as something I wanted to do myself.

Yesterday made it obvious.

I like deep conversations, but I also like making them understandable. If somebody uses a word I do not understand, I want to stop there. If somebody says the software ecosystem was different ten years ago, I want to know what "different" actually looked like. If somebody says distribution was difficult, I want the story of the first customer, the failed attempt, the thing that finally worked.

I am also getting more comfortable speaking. I used to become nervous simply joining Spaces. Hosting is starting to feel normal. I still lose words sometimes and I still think aloud before I reach the question, but I can feel that changing with repetition.

## One unfinished thread

One part of the conversation has stayed in my head. Edem mentioned inference engineering, and we also touched on AI engineering and people who seem unusually good at noticing where technology is moving before everybody else does.

I did not really know what inference engineering meant.

That made it interesting.

It opened a much bigger question for me: if AI is changing software work, what should somebody entering technology now actually be learning? Is this still a good time to study computer science? What should a student learn outside university? Which skills become more useful as AI gets better? And can somebody sitting in Accra realistically become world-class in one of these emerging areas?

Those are questions I want the next Tech Buddies conversation to explore.

I like that yesterday did not leave me with a neat conclusion. It left me with better questions. That is probably the point.
